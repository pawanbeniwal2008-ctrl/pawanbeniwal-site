Jan Samarth portal:what the data shows

One portal now fronts 16 central credit schemes. Its numbers are growing fast, and they leave some good questions open.

By Pawan Kumar Beniwal · 4 min read

Rural finance and livelihoods are a large part of my work, so I follow the Jan Samarth portal closely. It is the Government of India’s attempt to answer a simple question: how does a first-time borrower find out which loan scheme fits them? The Ministry of Finance has now published enough numbers to judge how far that answer has travelled.

What the portal does

The Prime Minister launched it on 6 June 2022 as a single window for credit-linked central schemes, with 13 schemes and 125-plus lenders, according to a PIB factsheet. An applicant answers a few eligibility questions and is matched with suitable schemes. They then apply online with basic details and documents, view offers from lenders, get digital approval from the bank they choose, and track the application. Livelihood loans under DAY-NRLM sat on it from the start.

What the numbers say

Two PIB releases give the picture. The first is dated 30 March 2026, with data as on 20 March. The second, a backgrounder for the portal’s fourth anniversary, is dated 5 June 2026, with data as on 1 June.

Applications processed grew from 41.14 lakh to 54.10 lakh, about a third more. Digital approvals went from 35.07 lakh beneficiaries to 49.55 lakh. Schemes rose from 15 to 16, and member lenders from 254 to 269, now including district central cooperative banks, regional rural banks and small finance banks alongside the big names.

The number I cannot explain

The amount is where it gets interesting. Applications worth Rs 1,06,306 crore became Rs 3,00,951 crore in 73 days, about 2.8 times, while the count grew by roughly 31%. By my arithmetic, that lifts the average from around Rs 26,000 an application to around Rs 56,000. The releases do not say why. The June list of schemes adds ECLGS 5.0 and a credit card for micro enterprises, but neither release gives scheme-wise figures, so I will not guess.

What is still missing

PIB reports digital approvals, which are not the same as money reaching an account. Neither release breaks the data down by state, by rural or urban borrower, or by women-led enterprise. PIB says the portal is offered in eight languages “for ease of access to rural and underprivileged population”. That is a good design choice, and it is also a claim the published data cannot yet test.

My takeaway

A portal widens the front door. Whether it reaches people at the margins depends on what happens after it: documents, branch follow-up and disbursement. Scheme-wise, state-wise and women-led data would show us that.

It is the same lesson as in my note on Delhi’s EV subsidy: early totals show who a programme reaches first, and the delivery work is widening that circle. The access question is also familiar from why farmers stay with wheat and rice.
